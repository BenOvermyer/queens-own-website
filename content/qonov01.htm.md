<p>
Heyla,
</p><p>
I am a Peace Corps volunteer who was evacuated from Kyrgyzstan.  This small and, largely unheard of, country lies just east of Uzbekistan, just south of Khazakhstan, and less than two hundred miles north of Afghanistan.  Although I left for Kyrgystan on June 6, Kyrgyzstan had become home for me and my wife.  We lived in a city called Naryn.  I
taught high school English (English as a Foreign Language) at a lyceum--a boarding school.  My wife was a consultant for a Non-governmental Organization support center (mainly supported by Counterpart Consortium).
</p><p>
Let me tell you a bit about the people I lived and worked with.  The national language is Kyrgyz, although the language used in government and business is Russian.  In Naryn, few people actually spoke Russian.  The city is one of the remotest cities in Kyrgyzstan.  My fellow English teachers were relatively small in size.  When I arrived on a site visit during training, they and my school's director took me to a mountain park called Salkin-Tor.  The picnic lasted from 11 in the morning until about 7 in the evening.  During this picnic we walked along mountain trails, ate traditional foods (Plov and Besh Barmok), and talked about everything from cultural differences to the purposes of having a language.
</p><p>
When school started, I taught high school grades 8 through 11 (they have no 12th grade).  My kids were very enthusiastic about learning English.  The classes were high energy.  I taught everything from simple sentences to the basics of literature.  The students absorbed everything about America as if they were dying of thirst and the information was the purest water.  When I started an English Club, students from other schools in the city came to practice their English, read books, play chess and other games, and just hang out.  It is difficult to describe the level of love and respect I felt from them.
</p><p>
My colleagues were fabulous.  They helped me get used to living in a country where the average income was roughly equivalent to $20/month.  When I was learning the language, they helped me with translations.  When I started teaching, they helped me learn school policies and procedures.  They helped me decorate my classroom.  In short, they became almost a second family.
</p><p>
When the tragedy of September 11th occurred, the whole school was called into the central square.  An announcement was made concerning the tragedy.  Fortunately, I had been told the night before by Peace Corps.  Everyone else was shocked.  For the longest time, I heard little else but condolences and good wishes.  But despite the tragedy, classes
continued.  Life, though temporarily subdued, went on.  English classes still had energy.  English club attracted more students every session.
</p><p>
Volunteers were bereaved about the tragedy.  It was almost incomprehensible.  From the safety of Kyrgyzstan, and there was no fear of personal safety from ANY volunteer, the tragedy was almost incomprehensible.  The event was seen as sublimely horrific.  Even so, we had absolutely no personal fear.  Depression abounded.  But when we were evacuated, we all felt intense bereavement.  We could not have been more disheartened if our families (in both countries) were dead.  It is difficult to tell who was hurt more after the evacuation--the volunteers or the locals at our sites.  In many ways, these people meant more to us than any other group of people.  They were even more horrified by the events than we were.  They mourned with us.  They comforted us.  They became friends so close, that leaving them was akin to dividing ourselves in half.
</p><p>
The second goal of Peace Corps is to share American culture with the host countries.  The third goal is to bring parts of the host countries to America.  The easiest part of being a Peace Corps Volunteer is going to a new country and sharing skills and knowledge.  The hardest part, in this situation, is to show people what the common person feels.  The new task of all the evacuated volunteers is to fight the inevitable prejudice and hard heartedness.  Nevertheless, this is our new assignment, and this letter is my first step along that path.
</p><p>
Sincerely,<br/>
Herald Belprin<br/>
